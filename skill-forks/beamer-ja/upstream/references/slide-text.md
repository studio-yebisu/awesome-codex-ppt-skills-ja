# Slide Text: Concise, Natural, and Specific

Use this guide before drafting or revising English, Chinese, or mixed-language slide text. It adapts prose-humanization principles to presentation constraints. The goal is not to make every slide casual or personal. The goal is to preserve the information the audience needs and remove language that sounds generic, inflated, repetitive, or written to fill a template.

## Preserve the Information Boundary

Do not remove or invent:

- facts, names, numbers, dates, units, citations, and source attribution;
- scope conditions, uncertainty, exceptions, and negative results;
- distinctions among observation, interpretation, decision, and plan;
- technical terms that the audience needs to understand the work.

Shortening is successful only when the remaining text still supports the same claim. If a missing detail is necessary, retrieve it from the source or ask the user. Do not replace it with a plausible generalization.

## Assign One Job to Each Text Region

- **Title:** state the question, finding, decision, or object of the slide. Prefer a concrete subject and verb when the slide makes a claim.
- **Section label:** locate the slide in the deck. Do not repeat the title.
- **Body:** provide the evidence, mechanism, comparison, or limitation needed to understand the title.
- **Figure labels:** identify objects directly. Keep them shorter than explanatory prose.
- **Caption or footer:** state source, scope, units, or an important reading condition. Do not use it for a second conclusion.

Remove a sentence that only announces the next point, restates the title, or tells the audience that the content is important.

## Prefer Concrete Slide Language

- Use simple verbs such as `is`, `has`, `uses`, `reduced`, `failed`, and `requires` when they are accurate.
- Name the actor when ownership matters: `The coordinator validates the checkpoint` is clearer than `Checkpoint validation is performed`.
- Replace broad praise or significance claims with the displayed fact.
- Name a source when the source is known. Remove unsupported phrases such as `experts suggest` or `industry reports show`.
- Keep qualifiers that change the claim. Remove qualifiers added only to soften an earlier overstatement.
- End on the last useful fact, limitation, or next action. Do not add a generic optimistic closing.

## Avoid Common AI-Like Slide Patterns

- inflated words such as `pivotal`, `groundbreaking`, `vital`, `rich`, `seamless`, or `transformative` without direct evidence;
- abstract stock phrases such as `the evolving landscape`, `at its core`, `the key insight`, or `what really matters`;
- `not only X but Y`, fake alternatives, or objections that no one raised;
- forced groups of three, mechanically parallel cards, or one bold mini-heading for every sentence;
- repeated `challenge / opportunity / future outlook` sections that add no specific fact;
- headings followed by a first sentence that merely repeats the heading;
- dramatic fragments, slogan-like endings, fake quotations, or decorative metaphors;
- chatbot traces such as greetings, reassurance, offers to continue, or knowledge-cutoff disclaimers;
- excessive bold, italics, dashes, parentheses, or color used as a substitute for hierarchy.

A repeated structure is acceptable when the content is a real comparison. Do not introduce an extra item only to make the layout symmetrical.

## English Slides

- Use sentence case for titles and headings unless an established deck or brand requires another convention.
- Prefer titles of roughly 4–10 words. A longer title is acceptable when the qualification is essential and the title region can hold it without shrinking.
- Use active voice when it clarifies who acted. Do not force active voice when the actor is irrelevant.
- Avoid long chains of nominalizations, stacked adjectives, and hyphenated modifiers.
- Use complete sentences for explanations. Short noun phrases are appropriate for node labels, table headings, and status tags.
- Set short prose and narrow text panels ragged-right. Avoid full justification and automatic hyphenation in headings.
- Vary sentence length naturally. Do not make every card use the same sentence pattern merely for symmetry.
- Read the slide aloud. It should sound like something the presenter could say without translating it into ordinary speech.

## Chinese Slides

- Prefer a direct topic or subject followed by the action, result, or condition. Remove bureaucratic openings and throat-clearing.
- Avoid repeated discourse markers equivalent to `moreover`, `therefore`, `meanwhile`, and `it is worth noting`. The layout usually provides enough transition.
- Do not force paired slogans, four-character rhythms, or three-part parallel structures when the source contains a simpler statement.
- Keep necessary qualifiers close to the claim they modify. Do not move all limitations into a vague final note.
- Use complete short clauses for explanations. Labels may be fragments, but several slogan fragments in sequence make technical content harder to follow.
- Use ragged-right paragraph composition. Do not accept automatic line breaks that split a technical term or create an implausible two-character fragment.
- Preserve established technical terms, Latin identifiers, mathematical notation, numbers, and units. Follow the deck's existing convention for spacing between Chinese and Latin text; never use manual spaces for alignment.
- A useful title is often about 8–18 Han characters, but meaning and rendered width take priority over the count.
- Read the slide aloud in Chinese. If the presenter would replace the written wording with a simpler sentence, revise the slide text.

## Mixed-Language Slides

- Choose one language for sentence grammar. Insert technical terms from the other language only where they are standard or more precise.
- Define an abbreviation once if the audience may not know it. Do not alternate between several translations of the same term.
- Do not duplicate every sentence in both languages unless the user requests a bilingual deck. Duplication usually harms hierarchy and available space.
- Check wrapping around Latin identifiers, formulas, parentheses, percentages, and units in the actual rendered slide.

## Density Heuristics

These are review prompts, not fixed limits:

- a visual-first slide often needs one title and two to four short observations;
- a narrow interpretation column should usually contain fewer than about 55 English words or 90 Han characters;
- if several paragraphs require the same emphasis and container treatment, the slide may contain more than one message;
- shortening a label is preferable to shrinking all text, but never shorten away a distinction required to interpret the visual.

## Text Review Pass

1. Write the slide's main message in one plain sentence.
2. Mark every fact, number, condition, term, and source that must survive editing.
3. Give the title, body, labels, and caption distinct jobs.
4. Remove meta narration, repetition, unsupported significance, stock transitions, and decorative symmetry.
5. Read the result aloud in the presentation language.
6. Render the slide and check that the edited wording still wraps naturally and leaves visible clearance.
7. Cross-check the final wording against the diagram, formula, table, and source material.
