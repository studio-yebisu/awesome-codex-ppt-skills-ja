# Content and narrative system

## Build the argument before the slides

Write the communication contract, audience shift, and central takeaway first. Then choose the narrative form that best fits the intent and evidence.

| Narrative form | Use when | Skeleton |
|---|---|---|
| Situation–Complication–Resolution–Example | A change or proposal must answer objections | stable context → tension → response → proof |
| Problem–Insight–Solution–Proof | A buyer or stakeholder must see a better approach | problem → non-obvious cause → mechanism → evidence |
| Question–Method–Result–Meaning | Research or technical evidence is primary | question → method → finding → implication |
| Decision–Criteria–Options–Recommendation | The audience owns a choice | decision → criteria → options → trade-offs → choice |
| Before–Choice–After–Learning | Work result or portfolio case | baseline → intervention → result → reflection |
| Vision–Antagonist–Hero–New world | Keynote or launch needs emotional momentum | possibility → status quo → solution → lived future |
| Teaching cycle | Capability, not persuasion, is the outcome | relevance → model → example → practice → feedback → transfer |

Use structure as a thinking tool, not a template that overrides the material.

## Claim ladder

For every substantive slide, fill this ladder:

1. **Audience question:** what are they silently asking here?
2. **Claim:** the answer in one complete sentence.
3. **Evidence:** the observable support.
4. **Meaning:** why the evidence matters.
5. **Transition:** what question naturally follows.

If the evidence cannot support the claim, weaken the claim, acquire better evidence, or remove the slide.

## Outline at three levels

1. **Act:** a major change in the audience's mental state.
2. **Sequence:** a group of slides resolving one question.
3. **Slide:** one communication job and one claim.

Use an analog storyboard or lightweight card list before layout when the deck is complex. Rearranging small units is cheaper than repairing finished slides.

## Takeaway titles

Write titles as audience-facing conclusions:

- Weak: `Market overview`
- Better: `Mid-market adoption is accelerating as implementation cost falls`

- Weak: `Our process`
- Better: `Three review gates keep scope changes visible before they become rework`

Keep titles concise enough to scan. Permit non-claim titles only for covers, section dividers, pauses, or branded closing slides. Avoid punctuation-heavy headlines, jargon chains, and vague labels.

## Evidence discipline

Classify each claim:

| Class | Required handling |
|---|---|
| Supplied fact | Point to file and page/slide/row/cell |
| External fact | Record URL, publisher, date, retrieval date, and claim supported |
| Calculation | Record inputs, formula, units, and rounding |
| Judgment | Attribute to the author/team or label as recommendation |
| Assumption | State it and describe impact if wrong |
| Scenario | Label visibly when it could be mistaken for actual performance |
| Quote/testimonial | Verify wording, speaker, permission, and context |

Do not infer causality from a trend alone. Do not compare metrics with different populations, time windows, currencies, or definitions without normalization or a warning.

## Source ledger behavior

- Give every usable claim or asset a stable ID such as `S01`, `S02`.
- Store the shortest location that lets another person verify it.
- Preserve original units and wording; record transformed wording separately.
- Track status: `verified`, `needs-check`, `scenario`, `excluded`, or `confidential`.
- Keep citations proportional to the delivery mode: visible footnote for decision-critical claims, concise source line for charts, full reference in notes or appendix.
- Never cite a search-results page when an underlying source exists.

## Research workflow

1. Read the user's material before searching.
2. Identify planning-critical gaps from the communication contract.
3. Search broad-to-narrow: entity/context → question → primary evidence.
4. Prefer official data, research papers, filings, standards, and first-party documentation.
5. Compare dates and definitions.
6. Save only facts that change the argument, design, or decision.
7. Update the ledger before writing slide copy.

## Writing for slides

- Use concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Replace abstraction with observable behavior or consequence.
- Put the conclusion before the explanation when the audience needs speed.
- Limit body copy to what the delivery mode can support.
- Use parallel grammar in comparisons and sequences.
- Avoid paragraphs in a live speaking deck.
- Do not bold everything; emphasize only the word or phrase needed for scanning.
- Replace labels such as `Problem`, `Solution`, and `Benefit` with content-bearing language when the structure is already clear.

## Notes are a separate channel

Use notes for:

- setup and transition language;
- evidence nuance, caveats, pronunciation, and source detail;
- demo steps and failure contingencies;
- audience interaction prompts;
- likely questions and answers;
- timing cues.

Do not copy the slide verbatim into notes. Do not hide decision-critical caveats exclusively in notes for an async deck.

## Audience journey and pacing

Use contrast in slide rhythm:

- dense evidence → synthesis;
- wide context → focused detail;
- tension → pause → resolution;
- explanation → example;
- claim → proof → implication.

Open deliberately. Useful openings include a decision, surprising verified fact, concrete scene, demonstrable problem, audience question, or future-state contrast. Avoid throat-clearing agenda slides unless orientation genuinely matters.

Close with resolution and action. A closing should answer: what should they remember, what should they do, who owns it, and by when? `Thank you` may appear, but it is not the argument's conclusion.

## Objection design

List the audience's strongest rational and emotional objections. Decide whether each belongs in:

- the main narrative before it is raised;
- a proof or risk slide;
- the appendix;
- speaker Q&A preparation.

Do not over-answer objections so early that the deck loses its main thread.

## Editing passes

Run distinct passes:

1. **Truth:** claims and sources.
2. **Logic:** sequence and missing links.
3. **Audience:** relevance, terminology, objections.
4. **Compression:** remove repetition and decorative detail.
5. **Voice:** concrete, consistent, and appropriate.
6. **Delivery:** on-slide copy versus notes versus appendix.

The deck is not ready when all source material is represented. It is ready when the necessary argument is clear and credible.
